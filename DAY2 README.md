# Data Analytics – Daily Task Update
## Day 2: Business Understanding, Analytical Thinking & Data Analysis

### 1. Data Analytics Life Cycle

The Data Analytics Life Cycle is a systematic process used to transform raw data into meaningful insights that help businesses make informed decisions and improve performance.

**Stages of the Data Analytics Life Cycle:**

1. **Understand the Business Problem:** Identify the challenge, business objective, and expected outcome.
2. **Collect Data:** Gather relevant information from databases, Excel files, APIs, surveys, and other sources.
3. **Clean Data:** Handle missing values, remove duplicates, correct errors, and standardize inconsistent formats.
4. **Analyze Data:** Examine data to identify patterns, trends, relationships, and performance gaps.
5. **Visualize Data:** Present findings using charts, graphs, KPI cards, and dashboards.
6. **Communicate Findings:** Explain the results, insights, and recommendations to stakeholders.
7. **Take Action:** Implement suitable business decisions based on the findings.
8. **Monitor Results:** Track KPIs and evaluate whether the actions have improved business performance.

**Example:** A company notices declining sales. A Data Analyst examines sales records, identifies possible reasons for the decline, recommends improvements, and monitors sales after the changes are implemented.

### 2. Business Problem

A Business Problem is a challenge or opportunity that affects a company's goals, customers, operations, revenue, or overall performance.

It describes **what the business wants to improve, understand, or solve**.

**Examples:**
- Monthly revenue has decreased.
- Customer retention is declining.
- Marketing expenses are increasing.
- Product returns are higher than expected.
- Employee productivity has decreased.

**Practical example:** A company's monthly revenue has dropped from ₹5 lakh to ₹3 lakh.

**Business question:** Why has revenue decreased, and what can the company do to improve it?

### 3. Data Problem

A Data Problem is the analytical question that must be answered using data to understand or solve a business problem.

It identifies the information needed, the data to examine, and the analysis required to investigate the business issue.

**Example:**

- **Business problem:** Monthly revenue has decreased from ₹5 lakh to ₹3 lakh.
- **Data problem:** Determine which products, regions, customer segments, or months contributed most to the revenue decline.
- **Required data:** Sales amount, order date, product category, region, customer ID, order status, and quantity sold.
- **Analysis:** Compare revenue across months, products, and regions to identify the main contributors.

**Key difference:** A business problem explains what is wrong or what needs improvement, while a data problem explains what must be investigated using data.

### 4. Metrics

A Metric is a measurable value used to track, quantify, or evaluate a particular activity or aspect of business performance.

Metrics help organizations understand what is happening in their operations.

**Examples:**
- Website visitors = 20,000
- Monthly revenue = ₹20 lakh
- Total orders = 1,500
- Customer complaints = 80
- Average delivery time = 3 days

**Common business metrics:**
- Revenue
- Profit
- Sales volume
- Customer acquisition cost
- Customer retention rate
- Conversion rate
- Average order value

**Example:** If a website receives 20,000 visitors in a month, the number of visitors is a metric that measures website traffic.

### 5. Key Performance Indicators (KPIs)

A Key Performance Indicator (KPI) is a measurable value selected to evaluate how effectively a business, team, or individual is achieving a specific objective.

A KPI is a strategically important metric connected to a business goal.

**Examples:**

- **Business goal:** Increase monthly revenue.  
  **KPI:** Monthly revenue growth rate.

- **Business goal:** Improve customer satisfaction.  
  **KPI:** Customer satisfaction score.

- **Business goal:** Increase website sales.  
  **KPI:** Website conversion rate.

- **Business goal:** Reduce delivery delays.  
  **KPI:** On-time delivery rate.

**Metric vs. KPI:**

- Metric: Total website visitors = 20,000.
- KPI: Website conversion rate = 4%, compared with a target of 5%.

A metric becomes a KPI when it is selected as a key measure of progress toward a defined business objective.

### 6. Descriptive Analysis

Descriptive Analysis explains **what happened** by summarizing historical or current data using totals, averages, percentages, charts, and other statistical measures.

It helps analysts understand past performance and identify basic trends.

**Example: Monthly Sales Analysis**

- January sales = ₹5 lakh
- February sales = ₹3 lakh
- March sales = ₹2 lakh

**Finding:** Revenue decreased by ₹2 lakh from January to March, representing a 60% decline.

**Formula for Percentage Change:**

Percentage Change = ((New Value − Old Value) / Old Value) × 100

In this example:

Percentage Change = ((2 − 5) / 5) × 100 = −60%

The negative result indicates a 60% decrease.

**Common techniques:**
- Sum and count
- Mean, median, and mode
- Percentage calculations
- Monthly and yearly comparisons
- Pivot tables and charts

**Tools:** Excel, SQL, Power BI, and Tableau.

### 7. Diagnostic Analysis

Diagnostic Analysis explains **why something happened** by investigating the factors, relationships, and possible causes behind an observed result.

It goes beyond describing a trend and examines what may have contributed to it.

**Example:** After identifying a decline in sales, the analyst investigates:

- Which products experienced lower sales?
- Did the number of customer orders decrease?
- Were prices or discounts changed?
- Did marketing campaigns generate fewer leads?
- Were products unavailable because of stock shortages?
- Did sales decline in a particular region?

**Possible finding:** The analysis may reveal that a product category experienced a significant drop in orders because of stock shortages.

**Important:** Diagnostic analysis can identify likely causes, but correlation alone does not prove causation.

### 8. Predictive Analysis

Predictive Analysis uses historical data, current information, statistical methods, and sometimes machine learning to estimate what might happen in the future.

It helps businesses forecast possible outcomes and prepare for future changes.

**Examples:**
- Forecasting next month's revenue.
- Predicting customer churn.
- Estimating future product demand.
- Predicting the probability of late deliveries.
- Forecasting inventory requirements.

**Practical example:** Based on historical sales trends and seasonal patterns, a model estimates that next month's revenue could be approximately ₹2.5 lakh.

This is a forecast, not a guaranteed result.

**Common techniques:** Regression, time-series forecasting, classification, and machine learning.

### 9. Prescriptive Analysis

Prescriptive Analysis recommends **what action should be taken** to achieve a desired outcome or improve a business situation.

It uses analytical findings, forecasts, business constraints, and sometimes optimization techniques to compare possible actions.

**Example:** Sales are declining, and the analysis indicates that product availability and low conversion rates may be contributing factors.

Possible recommendations include:

- Improve inventory planning.
- Test different marketing campaigns.
- Optimize product pricing.
- Improve the website checkout process.
- Focus on products with stronger profit margins.

The analyst evaluates which actions are most likely to improve results and recommends a suitable approach.

**Key difference:** Predictive analysis estimates what might happen, while prescriptive analysis recommends what should be done.

### 10. Four Types of Data Analysis

**Descriptive Analysis — What happened?**

Summarizes historical data to understand business performance.

Example: Monthly revenue decreased from ₹5 lakh to ₹2 lakh.

**Diagnostic Analysis — Why did it happen?**

Investigates the possible reasons behind an observed result.

Example: Sales declined because of stock shortages and fewer customer orders.

**Predictive Analysis — What might happen?**

Uses historical patterns and analytical models to forecast future outcomes.

Example: Forecasting next month's sales based on previous sales trends.

**Prescriptive Analysis — What should we do?**

Recommends actions to improve future performance.

Example: Improve stock availability and test targeted marketing campaigns.

**Memory trick:** What happened → Why did it happen → What might happen → What should we do?

### 11. Analytical Thinking

Analytical Thinking is the ability to break a complex business problem into smaller, manageable questions and use evidence and data to answer them logically.

It helps Data Analysts avoid assumptions and focus on measurable facts.

**Example:** A company experiences declining sales.

An analytical thinker asks:

1. When did sales begin to decline?
2. Which products experienced the largest decline?
3. Which regions or customer groups were affected?
4. Did website traffic or conversion rates change?
5. Were prices, promotions, or stock levels affected?
6. Which factors contributed most to the decline?
7. What actions could improve sales?

**Key skills:**
- Logical reasoning
- Problem decomposition
- Pattern recognition
- Hypothesis formation
- Evidence-based decision-making
- Root-cause investigation

### 12. Requirement Clarification

Requirement Clarification is the process of asking relevant questions to understand exactly what a client, manager, or stakeholder needs from an analysis or report.

It prevents misunderstandings and ensures that the final output answers the right business question.

**Important questions to ask:**

- What business problem are we trying to solve?
- What is the objective of the analysis?
- Which KPIs should be measured?
- What date range should be analyzed?
- Which departments, products, or regions are included?
- Where is the required data stored?
- Who will use the report?
- How frequently should the report be updated?
- What format is required: an Excel report, dashboard, or presentation?
- What business decision will the results support?

**Example:** If a manager requests a sales dashboard, clarify whether they need daily sales, monthly revenue, product-wise performance, regional comparisons, sales targets, or all of these.

**Objective:** Ensure that the analysis meets stakeholder expectations and supports a clear business decision.

### 13. Core Data Analytics Framework

The Core Data Analytics Framework is a sequence of questions that guides an analyst from identifying a problem to recommending an action.

**Step 1 — Business Question**

Ask: What problem are we solving?

Purpose: Define the business objective clearly.

**Step 2 — Define Metrics**

Ask: What numbers should we measure?

Purpose: Select relevant metrics and KPIs to evaluate performance.

**Step 3 — Identify Data**

Ask: What information do we need?

Purpose: Determine the data fields required to answer the business question.

**Step 4 — Collect Data**

Ask: Where can we obtain it?

Purpose: Gather relevant information from databases, Excel files, APIs, or other sources.

**Step 5 — Validate Data**

Ask: Can we trust the data?

Purpose: Check accuracy, completeness, consistency, and relevance.

**Step 6 — Clean Data**

Ask: What errors must be corrected?

Purpose: Handle missing values, duplicates, incorrect entries, and inconsistent formats.

**Step 7 — Analyze Data**

Ask: What is happening in the data, and why?

Purpose: Identify trends, patterns, relationships, and possible causes.

**Step 8 — Visualize Data**

Ask: How can we present the results clearly?

Purpose: Use charts, graphs, reports, and dashboards to communicate findings.

**Step 9 — Generate Insights**

Ask: What did we learn?

Purpose: Explain the meaning and business implications of the analysis.

**Step 10 — Recommend Actions**

Ask: What should we do?

Purpose: Suggest practical, evidence-based improvements.

**Step 11 — Make Business Decisions**

Ask: What will the company actually implement?

Purpose: Help stakeholders select an appropriate course of action.

**Step 12 — Monitor Results**

Ask: Did the action work?

Purpose: Track KPIs, evaluate the impact, and adjust the approach when necessary.

### 14. Practical Business Case Study: Declining Sales

**Scenario:** A retail company has observed that its revenue has decreased over three months.

- January revenue = ₹5,00,000
- February revenue = ₹3,00,000
- March revenue = ₹2,00,000

**Step 1 — Identify the Business Problem**

Revenue has declined significantly over three months.

**Step 2 — Define the Business Question**

Why are sales decreasing, and how can revenue be improved?

**Step 3 — Define Metrics and KPIs**

- Metric: Monthly revenue.
- KPI: Monthly revenue growth rate compared with a defined target.
- Additional metrics: Order volume, average order value, and product-wise revenue.

**Step 4 — Identify and Collect Data**

Gather order dates, revenue, product categories, quantities, customer details, discounts, and inventory records.

**Step 5 — Validate and Clean Data**

Check for duplicate orders, missing values, cancelled transactions, and inconsistent records.

**Step 6 — Perform Descriptive Analysis**

Revenue declined from ₹5 lakh in January to ₹2 lakh in March, a 60% decrease.

**Step 7 — Perform Diagnostic Analysis**

Compare product performance, customer orders, stock availability, discounts, and regional sales to investigate possible causes.

**Step 8 — Perform Predictive Analysis**

Forecast future revenue using historical trends, seasonality, and relevant business factors.

**Step 9 — Perform Prescriptive Analysis**

Recommend suitable actions, such as improving stock availability or testing targeted promotions, based on the findings.

**Step 10 — Make a Business Decision**

Management selects the actions that best fit its budget, objectives, and operational constraints.

**Step 11 — Monitor Results**

Track revenue, conversion rate, order volume, and profit to evaluate whether the actions are effective.

### 15. Day 2 — Key Takeaways

- **Data Analytics Life Cycle:** A structured process for turning data into business value.
- **Business Problem:** The challenge or opportunity the company wants to address.
- **Data Problem:** The question that data analysis must investigate.
- **Metric:** A measurable value used to track an activity or result.
- **KPI:** A key metric linked to an important business objective.
- **Descriptive Analysis:** Explains what happened.
- **Diagnostic Analysis:** Investigates why it happened.
- **Predictive Analysis:** Estimates what might happen.
- **Prescriptive Analysis:** Recommends what should be done.
- **Analytical Thinking:** Breaks complex problems into smaller, evidence-based questions.
- **Requirement Clarification:** Ensures the analyst understands stakeholder expectations.
- **Core Framework:** Guides the analysis from the business question through monitoring the outcome.

**Day 2 Learning Objective:** Learn to translate a business problem into measurable questions, identify the right data, select suitable analytical methods, and communicate recommendations that support informed business decisions.
